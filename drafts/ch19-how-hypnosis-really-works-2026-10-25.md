CHAPTER 19 — HOW HYPNOSIS REALLY WORKS

If you use any suggestion in your show at all, a PK touch, a hand that will not open, even a line like "you will find this word harder to let go of than you expect," you have probably had two conversations afterward. One person wants to know if you can make their husband quit smoking, and the other one, usually with their arms folded, wants you to know that hypnosis is not real. I get both, and for a long time I handled the second one badly, because I either got a little mystical about it or I shrugged and said it was basically a trick. Both answers lose you the person you most want to keep, which is the skeptic several rows back who clocks everything. This chapter is what I say now. It is hypnosis the way I would explain it to an investigator who took my training and then came to see my show, and after that it is how I actually use it on stage.

WHAT IT FEELS LIKE

You already know what hypnosis feels like from the inside, because you have been in something very much like it hundreds of times without anybody swinging a watch at you. You arrive at your exit on the highway and realize you have no memory of the last several miles, even though you responded to every light and made every lane change correctly. Or you are reading a book, and you get to the bottom of the page and realize you have not taken in a single word, because your attention was completely somewhere else. Psychologists have a word for that quality of getting pulled all the way into something, and it is absorption (the term comes from Auke Tellegen, who built the questionnaire that measures it in the 1970s). Whether we should also call it a trance is a different question, and honestly it is a sixty year old argument that I will get to in a minute. What matters here is that the raw ingredient is ordinary. Nobody needs a script or a special state in order to lose the room around them. People do it every day.

Why People Find Hypnosis Hard to Believe

I have always found it strange that people doubt hypnosis while accepting something far more bizarre without a second thought, which is dreaming. Every night, human beings close their eyes in a quiet room and enter fully immersive realities generated entirely by their own brain. There are people in those worlds, and we feel real fear and real joy in there, with no chemicals and no props. Nobody calls that pseudoscience. We call it sleeping. Also, the dreaming brain will happily fold in whatever is going on around you. A sound in the room becomes part of the story without any resistance at all (sleep researchers tested this decades ago by spraying water on sleeping volunteers and asking what they had dreamed, and the water showed up in the dreams). To me, hypnosis is the same machinery pointed somewhere on purpose. The brain has always been able to take expectation, attention, memory, emotion and suggestion and turn them into something that feels immediate and real. The only part that feels foreign is that this time the suggestion is coming from somebody else.

A Cold War Memo That Stayed With Me

I would love to tell you I found the following quote in a vault. I found it the same way you can, which is in the CIA's online reading room, in a six page memo titled "Hypnotism and Covert Operations" that was written on May 5, 1955 and declassified in 1999. It says this:

"Frankly, I now distrust much of what is written by academic experts on hypnotism. Partly this is because many of them appear to have generalized from a very few cases; partly because much of their cautious pessimism is contradicted by Agency experimenters; but more particularly because I personally have witnessed behavior responses which respected experts have said are impossible to obtain. In no other field have I been so conscious of the mental claustrophobia of book and lecture hall knowledge. I don't think we have enough evidence to say positively that hypnotism is a practicable covert weapon, but I do say that we'll never know whether it is or not unless we experiment in the field where we can learn what is practicable materially and psychologically in a way that no laboratory worker could possibly prove."

What struck me most was the tone. During the Cold War, intelligence people took hypnosis seriously enough to study it for interrogation and behavior control, and the author is clearly frustrated that the academics were too cautious to see what he thought he had seen in the field. I think it is worth knowing that the people who were paid to find out whether hypnosis was a weapon were not fantasists, and it is just as worth knowing how the story ended. It did not become a weapon. Within a few years the medical world was taking it seriously for a completely different reason. The British Medical Association endorsed its medical use in 1955, and the American Medical Association followed in 1958, saying hypnosis had a recognized place in medicine when used by qualified people. That is the real arc of the twentieth century on this subject. It went from the fringe of covert operations to the mainstream of pain clinics, and the reason that happened is that it works in a specific and measurable way, which is what the rest of this chapter is about.

What Hypnosis Actually Is, and the Argument Behind It

Here is the thing most hypnosis books leave out. Researchers have been arguing for sixty years about whether hypnosis is a special state of consciousness at all. On one side you have the state theorists, Ernest Hilgard at Stanford in the last century and David Spiegel, also at Stanford, today, who hold that something real changes in the brain when a responsive person is hypnotized. On the other side you have the sociocognitive theorists, Nicholas Spanos, Irving Kirsch and Steven Jay Lynn among them, who argue that everything we call hypnosis can be explained by suggestion, expectation, motivation and the role a person has agreed to play, with no special state required. In 2014 the American Psychological Association's hypnosis division tried to write a definition both camps could live with, and they landed on "a state of consciousness involving focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness characterized by an enhanced capacity for response to suggestion." Even that got attacked in print by the sociocognitive side as having no evidence behind it. I tell you this because when a skeptic says hypnosis is not real, they are usually half remembering this argument, and the honest answer is that the state is genuinely contested and the responsiveness is not. The thing that is measured, replicated and stable is how much a given person responds to suggestion. Argue about the word trance all you like. Hypnotizability is as real as height.

Hypnotizability Is the Real Thing

Hypnotizability is measured with standardized scales, and the one researchers trust most is the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale, Form C. It is twelve graded suggestions, from an arm getting heavy, to hands drifting together on their own, to hearing a mosquito that is not in the room, to not being able to remember what happened until a cue word is given. Scores form a bell curve. The average person gets around five or six of the twelve, and the people at the very top, scoring ten or more, are under one in ten of the population, which is where the "about one in ten are highly hypnotizable" figure comes from (you will see fifteen percent quoted as well, and that number comes from looser cutoffs and other scales).

The part I find remarkable is how stable it is. In 1989 Piccione, Hilgard and Zimbardo published a study of fifty people who had been tested as Stanford students between 1958 and 1962, then again in 1970, then again in 1985. The correlation between the first test and the twenty five year retest was .71, and the typical person's score had moved by one point on a twelve point scale. That is about as stable as anything psychologists measure about a person. If somebody is a strong responder in college, they are a strong responder at fifty.

Who Responds, and Why Absorption Is Not the Answer You Think

Everybody wants a personality type to look for, and the one that gets quoted is absorption, the trait I mentioned above, the tendency to get completely lost in a movie or a piece of music. It is real, and it is the best known predictor, and it is also weak. The correlation with hypnotizability runs around .2 to .3 in most studies, and it depends on the setting (Amanda Barnier and Kevin McConkey found a correlation of .24 when absorption was measured in an imaginative setting and .09 when the same questionnaire was handed out in a classroom). John Kihlstrom, who has spent his career on this, puts it plainly: it is too weak to predict any one person. Agreeableness, by the way, predicts nothing at all, and neither does being a people pleaser. The person nodding hardest in the front row is not your subject for that reason.

What predicts better is expectation. In 1999 Wayne Braffman and Irving Kirsch measured students on suggestibility with and without a hypnotic induction, along with their expectations, their motivation, their absorption and their fantasy proneness, and once everything else was accounted for, the one thing that still predicted response was what the person expected to happen. Together, their expectation, their motivation and their plain suggestibility without any hypnosis explained about half of how they behaved under hypnosis. So the practical order is this. Look for the person who gets lost in things, because it is a start. Then confirm it with a quick suggestibility test (the hand lock, the magnetic fingers, the pendulum, any of the ones described in the contact mind reading section), because a thirty second test tells you more than any trait does. And then build their expectation, because that is the lever you actually control.

The Induction Adds Less Than You Think

This is the finding that changed how I think about my own pre-show. The same Braffman and Kirsch work, and a 2008 study by Kirsch's group with the great title "Slipping into trance," found that people respond to the same suggestions nearly as well without any induction as with one. Responses with and without the induction were highly correlated, the induction added only a small bump on average, and a notable minority of people actually responded less after being formally hypnotized. Hypnotic responsiveness is still its own thing, in that it barely correlates with how suggestible a person is under interrogation or how strongly they respond to a placebo (Anthony Tasso and Nicole Pérez lay this out in the Oxford Handbook of Hypnosis), but it is very nearly the same thing as how they respond to imaginative suggestions in a normal waking conversation.

To me, this means the induction is mostly permission and expectation. When you tell somebody to close their eyes and breathe, you are not flipping a switch in their brain. You are telling them what kind of experience they are about to have and giving them permission to have it. Peter Turner made this point to me about contact mind reading, and it applies here word for word. The line "you will not consciously know you are doing this" sounds like a description of what is about to happen, and it is really a suggestion, and it is doing a lot of the work.

The Wording Decides Which Part of the Brain Does the Work

If you want one study that proves suggestion is specific rather than a vague relaxation effect, it is Pierre Rainville's. In 1997 his group in Montreal published a study in Science in which people under hypnosis put a hand in painfully hot water while they were scanned. The suggestions were written to change how unpleasant the pain felt without changing how intense it felt, and that is exactly what happened in the scanner. Activity changed in the anterior cingulate cortex, the part of the brain that attaches feeling and urgency to a sensation, and did not change in the somatosensory cortex, the strip across the top of the brain that maps where on the body a sensation is and how strong it is. Four years later Robert Hofbauer and the same group ran it the other way, with suggestions about intensity instead, and this time the somatosensory cortex changed. The authors call it a double dissociation, which is the scientist's way of saying that the words chose the brain region. For us the lesson is very practical. Write the suggestion for the exact experience you want the person to have, because a suggestion about heaviness and a suggestion about calm are not interchangeable, and the brain treats them differently.

What the Brain Scans Show, and What They Do Not

I want to be careful here, because this is where most books on hypnosis start lying by enthusiasm. There are two brain findings I am comfortable repeating. In 2012 Fumiko Hoeft and David Spiegel's group at Stanford scanned twelve highly hypnotizable people and twelve low ones at rest, doing nothing, and found that the highs had a stronger connection between the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which is the part of the brain that holds a goal in mind, and the dorsal anterior cingulate, which belongs to the salience network, the system that decides what matters right now. The structure of their brains did not differ. Their networks were simply wired to talk to each other more. Then in 2017 Heidi Jiang and the same group scanned fifty seven people picked from 545 they screened, this time during hypnosis, and found that in the highs the cingulate quieted down, the prefrontal cortex coupled more tightly with the insula (the region that tracks what is going on inside your body), and it coupled less tightly with the default mode network, which is the network that runs your ongoing commentary about yourself. That fits what hypnosis feels like from the inside, with the attention narrowed, the body loud and the inner narrator turned down.

Here is what I will not say, and what I would ask you not to say either. Nobody has found a brain signature of being hypnotized. In 2017 Mathieu Landry, Michael Lifshitz and Amir Raz pooled every brain imaging study of hypnosis they could find and looked for what replicated across all of them, and almost nothing did. The one region that survived was the lingual gyrus, a visual area involved in mental imagery. So when somebody tells you a scan proves trance is real, they are ahead of the data, and when somebody tells you scans prove hypnosis is fake, they are behind it. The honest sentence is that the replicated brain findings are about who is hypnotizable and about attention, and they are not about a switch.

Why It Feels Like It Is Happening by Itself

The strangest thing about hypnosis, to the person experiencing it, is that their arm lifts and they feel like they did not lift it. The cleanest explanation I know comes from Zoltan Dienes and Josef Perner, and it is called cold control theory. The idea is that the person really is performing the action, intentionally, the way they would perform any action. What is missing is the thought about the thought, what philosophers call a higher-order thought, the one that would normally say "I am doing this on purpose." Take that away and the action feels like it came from somewhere else. That is also why the best evidence for hypnosis changing how people think is so clean. In 2002 Amir Raz and Michael Posner's group gave highly hypnotizable people a posthypnotic suggestion that the words they were about to see were meaningless symbols from a foreign language, then ran them through the Stroop test, the one where the word RED is printed in green ink and you have to name the ink color. The interference that slows everybody down, every single time, basically disappeared in the highs. They were not pretending. They were doing something with their attention that most of us cannot do on request, and the suggestion gave them a way to do it.

Where the Swinging Watch Comes From

Think about the last time you rode in the back of a car with your eyes closed, not asleep, just letting the trip happen to you. If it was daytime the sun was strobing through the trees across your closed lids in a slow uneven rhythm, and if it was night the streetlights were sliding past one after another doing the same thing, and somewhere in the middle of all that gentle flicker you got heavy and warm and almost helplessly sleepy. There is a real mechanism under that, and it is simpler than the one most hypnotists reach for. A slow flicker does pull the brain's electrical rhythm toward its own pace (the EEG people call it photic driving, and it shows up in somewhere between half and eighty percent of people). However, there is no study showing that this flicker makes anyone sleepy or more suggestible, and the story you sometimes hear about flicker imitating the eye movements of dreaming sleep does not hold up, because the eyes at the edge of sleep roll slowly, and rapid eye movement does not arrive until an hour or more into the night. What is actually putting you under in that back seat is monotony. The vigilant part of your attention has nothing new to track, so it stops standing guard, and your eyes are tired, and you expected to doze.

The swinging watch is the same recipe done on purpose. James Braid, the Scottish surgeon who gave us the word hypnotism in the 1840s, found that if you have somebody stare at a small bright object held a little above eye level, their eyes and lids tire quickly and close on their own, and the fixed attention does the rest. So the cartoon was never nonsense. It just had a boring explanation: one monotonous thing to track, a pair of tired eyes, a steady voice, and a person who came in expecting to go under. Once you know that, you do not need the watch. A voice with a repeating cadence does the same job, and so does a slow gesture repeated until they stop noticing it, or one point for the eyes to rest on.

What This Means for a Stage Show

Stage hypnosis, including the small pieces of suggestion most of us use, is hypnotizability plus compliance plus role, and I think we should be honest with ourselves about all three. The group suggestibility test at the top of a hypnosis show is doing selection, whatever it looks like from the seats, because it finds the one in ten responders in the audience in two minutes. The social situation does a great deal of the rest. A volunteer who walked up in front of three hundred people has agreed to a role, and people are very good at playing roles they have accepted (Graham Wagstaff and Nicholas Spanos built careers on showing how much of a hypnosis show is exactly this). None of that makes it fake. It is the same machinery of expectation and permission that the lab studies describe, running in a room with the volume up.

Two boundaries, and I mean both of them. I do not make therapeutic claims from the stage, because the clinical evidence is strongest for pain and for anxiety around medical procedures and gets thinner from there, and the person who wants me to cure her husband's smoking deserves a referral rather than a trick. Also, I never let hypnosis anywhere near a claim about interrogation or truth. Hypnotically refreshed testimony is excluded or restricted in most American courts for good reason, and the investigators I train would rightly lose respect for me if I implied otherwise. Like with all of the real material in this book, the rule is the one I state at the top of my show: when what I am doing is real, I will tell you, and I do not claim a false method as a real one.

Chris Michael's Take

Expectation is half the induction, and the research backs that up better than it backs almost anything else in this chapter. So for me the hypnosis starts in the pre-show, in how the piece is framed and in the language around it, long before anyone closes their eyes. When I pick a volunteer I look for the person who gets lost in a movie, and then I confirm it with a quick test rather than trusting the look, because the look is a weak predictor and the test is not. And I keep every suggestion short and specific, because Rainville taught me that the wording chooses which part of the brain does the work, and a vague suggestion gets a vague result.

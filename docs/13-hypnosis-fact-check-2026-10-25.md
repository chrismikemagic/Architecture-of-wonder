# Chapter 19 fact-check: How Hypnosis Really Works (2026-10-25)

Every scientific claim in Chapter 19 (manuscript-extracted.txt lines 2235 to 2329) checked against the published literature. Line numbers refer to the current extracted manuscript. Verdicts: HOLDS, HOLDS WITH A FIX, OVERSTATED, UNSUPPORTED, WRONG.

## Claim by claim

| Lines | Claim | Verdict | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2239-2240 | Highway driving and reading without retaining are "the everyday experience of trance" and "what hypnosis feels like from the inside" | HOLDS WITH A FIX | The everyday analogue is absorption (Tellegen), and it is real. "Trance" is the contested word. The sociocognitive school (Spanos, Kirsch, Lynn) denies any special state; the APA Division 30 definition of 2014 was written to stay neutral ("a state of consciousness involving focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness characterized by an enhanced capacity for response to suggestion", Elkins, Barabasz, Council and Spiegel 2015), and Lynn, Green, Kirsch and colleagues attacked even that as having no empirical foundation. Say absorption, and name the debate. |
| 2242-2246 | The dreams argument | HOLDS | Rhetorical, and the incorporation of outside sounds into dreams is documented (Dement and Wolpert 1958). |
| 2248 | "I first encountered the following quote through my intelligence work, and I am at liberty to share it here" | WRONG FRAMING | The quote is from the CIA memo "Hypnotism and Covert Operations", dated 5 May 1955, released under FOIA on 4 October 1999 and sitting in the CIA Reading Room. It has been public for 27 years. Say "a declassified 1955 CIA memo". The quoted text matches the document. |
| 2251 | Hypnosis "still comes up in a surprisingly normal and matter-of-fact way in conversations about interrogation, influence, interviewing" | UNSUPPORTED | Anecdote presented as the state of a field. Hypnosis in interviewing is legally radioactive: hypnotically refreshed witness testimony is excluded or restricted in most US jurisdictions (Rock v. Arkansas, 1987, protected only a defendant's own testimony). Cut or restate as your own experience. |
| 2252 | Medical organizations began recognizing its value | HOLDS | British Medical Association report, BMJ, April 1955; American Medical Association report, JAMA, 13 September 1958 ("a recognized place in the medical armamentarium"). The AMA has no current position, its pre-1987 policies were sunset. |
| 2253, 2260 | "Modern neuroscience gives us a much clearer picture", "you can see this in imaging studies, the networks actually change" | OVERSTATED | Landry, Lifshitz and Raz 2017 (Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews 81) meta-analysed the imaging literature and found few reliable patterns across studies; the only region that survived was the lingual gyrus. The balanced reviews are Oakley and Halligan 2013 (Nature Reviews Neuroscience 14) and Terhune, Cleeremans, Raz and Lynn 2017 (same NBR issue). The best-replicated findings are about who is hypnotizable, not about a hypnotic state. |
| 2257 | The anterior cingulate "shows reduced activity in highly hypnotizable individuals" | HOLDS WITH A FIX | Jiang, White, Greicius, Waelde and Spiegel (Cerebral Cortex 27(8), 2017; online 2016): during hypnosis, in 57 highs and lows selected from 545 screened, dorsal ACC activity fell. Add "during hypnosis". One study. |
| 2257 | The DLPFC "shifts how it communicates with the rest of the network" | HOLDS | Same study: DLPFC to insula connectivity rose, DLPFC to posterior cingulate (default mode) connectivity fell. |
| 2257 | The insula "begins weighing suggested experience more heavily" | OVERSTATED | An interpretation of the connectivity result, not a measured finding. |
| 2257, 2263 | The default mode network "quiets down in ways that resemble deep meditation"; "one of the most replicated ideas" | OVERSTATED | McGeown, Mazzoni, Venneri and Kirsch 2009 (Consciousness and Cognition) found reduced anterior default mode activity in highs after induction; Jiang 2017 found reduced DLPFC to PCC coupling. Other studies found increases. "Several studies", not "most replicated", and drop the meditation comparison. |
| 2258-2259 | Hypnosis narrows attention, reduces self-monitoring, changes top-down weighting | HOLDS AS A MODEL | This is the top-down regulation framing of Terhune et al. 2017. Say it is a model, not a measurement. |
| 2266 | In highs, stronger interaction between DLPFC and dorsal ACC | HOLDS | Hoeft et al. 2012 (Archives of General Psychiatry 69(10)): at rest, 12 highs vs 12 lows, greater left DLPFC to salience network (dACC) connectivity; grey and white matter did not differ. A trait difference at rest, n of 24. |
| 2270-2273 | Rainville: suggestion for unpleasantness changed ACC, not S1; related work changed S1 with intensity suggestions | HOLDS | Rainville, Duncan, Price, Carrier and Bushnell, Science 277, 1997. Hofbauer, Rainville, Duncan and Bushnell, Journal of Neurophysiology 86, 2001. The authors call it a double dissociation. This is the strongest paragraph in the chapter. |
| 2275 | Tasso and Pérez, Oxford Handbook of Hypnosis | HOLDS AS A CITATION | Chapter 11, "Parsing everyday suggestibility: what does it tell us about hypnosis?", Nash and Barnier eds., 2008, pp. 283-310. |
| 2275 | Hypnotic responsiveness is distinguishable from everyday suggestibility | HALF RIGHT | True of interrogative, placebo and sensory suggestibility, which barely correlate with hypnotizability. Not true of imaginative suggestibility: responses to the same suggestions with and without an induction are highly correlated and the induction adds only a small increase (Braffman and Kirsch 1999, JPSP 77(3); Kirsch et al. 2008, "Slipping into trance"). For a performer this is the important fact: most of the response is available with no induction at all. |
| 2275 | The predicting trait is "primarily absorption" | OVERSTATED | Absorption is the best-known trait correlate and a weak one, around .2 to .3, and context-dependent (Council, Kirsch and Hafner 1986; Nadon et al. 1991 reaffirmed it; Barnier and McConkey 1999 found .24 in an imagination setting and .09 in a classroom). Kihlstrom: too weak to predict an individual. Expectancy is the stronger predictor (Braffman and Kirsch: the only unique predictor of nonhypnotic suggestibility; with motivation and nonhypnotic suggestibility it explained about half the variance in behavioural response to hypnosis). |
| 2275 | Compliance, agreeableness, susceptibility to flattery predict almost nothing | HOLDS | Big Five correlations with hypnotizability are near zero. |
| 2276 | Roughly 10 to 15 percent of adults are highly hypnotizable | HOLDS WITH A CAVEAT | Depends on scale and cutoff. "Virtuosos" scoring 10 to 12 on the Stanford Form C are under 10 percent (Register and Kihlstrom 1986); the distribution is roughly normal, mean 5.6 of 12 (Hilgard 1965); 15 percent appears with looser cutoffs. Say "about one in ten". |
| 2276 | Hypnotizability is stable over decades | HOLDS | Piccione, Hilgard and Zimbardo 1989 (JPSP 56): 50 people tested as students 1958 to 1962, retested 1970 and 1985; r = .64 at 10 years, .82 at 15, .71 at 25; median individual change one point on a 12-point scale. |
| 2276 | "Pick for absorption" | HOLDS AS ADVICE, FIX THE REASON | Absorption is a weak predictor; the practical screen is the one you already use, a suggestibility test, plus the person's expectation that it will work. |
| 2278-2279 | The Alpha Shift: beta to alpha makes the brain "significantly less resistant to incoming suggestion"; a three-step process "reliably produces this shift" | UNSUPPORTED | Eyes closed and relaxed shifts the EEG toward alpha (the Berger effect), which only means the person is relaxed. No study links alpha to reduced resistance to suggestion. The EEG hypnosis literature points to theta, with mixed results (Jensen, Adachi and Hakimian 2015, American Journal of Clinical Hypnosis 57; a 2021 Heliyon study found no theta marker in highs). The "reliable three-step process" is not a finding. Delete the section. |
| 2281-2287 | Swinging watch: flicker through closed lids "echoes what the brain does at the edge of sleep", REM named for it, feeding it back nudges the hypnagogic state | MIXED | Holds: Braid's 1843 method, fixed gaze on a bright object held above eye level, tires the lids and closes the eyes; flicker entrains the EEG at the flicker frequency (photic driving, Adrian and Matthews 1934; 50 to 80 percent of people show it). Wrong: sleep onset is marked by slow rolling eye movements, not REM, which arrives 70 to 90 minutes later; no controlled study links flicker entrainment to drowsiness or hypnotic depth. The honest mechanism is monotony and habituation (an unchanging stimulus stops being tracked), eye fatigue from fixation, and expectation. Keep the car window as the image, drop the REM paragraph. |
| 2289-2295 | Down to the cell level: pyramidal neurons, GABAergic interneurons, excitation and inhibition | HOLDS AS TEXTBOOK, NOT AS HYPNOSIS RESEARCH | There is no cell-level hypnosis research in humans; the chapter admits this. The section is extrapolation from predictive processing (Martin and Pacherie 2019 give the predictive-coding account of hypnotic agency). Cut. |
| 2297-2302 | Neuromodulators: norepinephrine gain, acetylcholine, dopamine | HOLDS AS GENERAL NEUROSCIENCE, SPECULATIVE FOR HYPNOSIS | Locus coeruleus gain (Aston-Jones and Cohen 2005) and acetylcholine and attention are standard; their role in hypnosis is inference, as the text says. Dopamine: the COMT Val158Met studies disagree (Raz 2006 one pattern, Katonai 2017 the opposite, Presciuttini 2014 and Bryant 2013 null); "less settled" is right. Cut the section. |
| 2305-2308 | Pain pathways and the ACC | HOLDS | Melzack and Casey's sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational split; ACC input from medial thalamus; ACC plasticity in pain from animal work. ## What a person who explains hypnosis scientifically needs to be able to say

1. **There are two camps and a neutral definition.** State theorists (Hilgard, Spiegel) and sociocognitive theorists (Spanos, Kirsch, Lynn) have argued for sixty years about whether hypnosis is a special state. The 2014 APA definition is the truce. Know both.
2. **Hypnotizability is the real, stable thing.** It is measured (Stanford Form C, Harvard Group Scale, Elkins scale), roughly normal, about one in ten at the top, and about as stable over 25 years as IQ.
3. **The induction adds little.** Most of the response to a suggestion is there without any induction. What the induction and the label add is expectation and permission.
4. **Expectancy is the strongest lever you control.** It predicts response better than any personality trait, including absorption.
5. **Wording is specific.** Rainville and Hofbauer: change the words and a different part of the brain changes.
6. **There is no brain signature of "being hypnotized".** Landry 2017. The replicated brain findings (Hoeft 2012, Jiang 2017) are about highs versus lows and about attention and salience networks, not a switch.
7. **Involuntariness is explainable.** Cold control: the person does it on purpose without the thought "I am doing this on purpose".
8. **Stage hypnosis is hypnotizability plus compliance plus role.** The group suggestibility test selects highs; social pressure and role enactment do the rest (Wagstaff 1981; Spanos). This is exactly Peter Turner's point in the contact mind reading chapter.
9. **The clinical evidence is strongest for pain and procedural anxiety**, weaker for smoking and weight. Do not overclaim therapy.
10. **The CIA did look at it (1955 memo), and it did not become a weapon.** Say so as history, from a public document.

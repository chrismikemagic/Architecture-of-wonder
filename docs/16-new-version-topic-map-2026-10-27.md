## The five parts

### Part One: what the audience is scoring

Chris as the subject of observation. The four domains (comfort, emphasis, synchrony, perception management) applied to the performer, which is the Sapien article. Valence as the scoreboard and "neutral is negative", from the Business Course. The first impression before hello (the mouthwash candidate, the judge who noticed Joe standing for the jury). Angles rather than face to face: Joe sits beside people, and the reservation case is the story. Shake and wait at the walk-up. Hands visible, pointing with the open hand, the two-button jacket, all from his mock-jury work. Gravity-defying emphasis, say it then look, stakes over conditions, and the liar's set to cut from every script. Temporal control: whoever controls time controls, the twenty-second silence, never answering instantly, applied to the reveal and to Q&A. "Perform it until it is hardwired" as the practice doctrine. The wall push and "I'm nervous" as pre-show rituals. The resting contempt face and its remedy for the listening face while a volunteer talks.

### Part Two: face time

The show as an interaction rather than a presentation. Face time is the goal, from interrogation to the stage. Assess, engage, transact mapped onto pre-show, the walk-up and the reveal, with the spy walk as the story for synchrony before any ask, which is Chris's rapport-before-the-propless-read rule. Isopraxis, verbal mirroring with their exact words, and the Schafer and Navarro rapport tools Chris picked out: common ground, vicarious experience, ask a favor, the compliment that lets them praise themselves. The hassle test and the comfort dividend applied to the event itself: the coordinator, the room layout, the walk from seat to stage. Angled feet open a strolling group, the standing leg cross buys face time, feet toward the exit means release them. Chronicity and Chris's afternoon-slump observation. The influence order, visual then vocal then verbal, which is why the entrance outweighs the script.

**Rapport and likability (added 2026-10-27).** The chapter every performer thinks they already know, and probably the one that sells the book.

- The eyebrow flash. Joe's "greet with your eyes, they never fail", the flash as the universal greeting, synchrony as harmony (mirror the greeting, the flash, the pace, their exact words), and the brow flash as the emphasis cue in storytelling.
- The angle. Joe stood at an angle with slow open palms at his first crime scene. He moved his chair beside the mother in the reservation case. He talks to a child at an angle with a tilted head rather than square on like a drill instructor, and the tilt buys face time. Face to face limits cooperation.
- Men versus women, stated the way Joe states it. Men approach men at a slight angle and connect side by side over a shared activity. Women generally prefer a direct approach with more space, then rotate to an angle once they are comfortable. His own surveys had women asking men to stand about a foot farther back. So "never in front of somebody" is the rule for men and the ending position for women. Handshake rules (light pressure, fingers down, step back and let them set the distance) and shake and wait as the first read. Touch only forearm or elbow, with consent.
- Likability itself. What people say they like about Joe is that he is approachable. The three A's (awareness, amiability, acceptance). The twenty behaviors that make people seem untrustworthy as the performer's don't list. The resting contempt face and its remedy (lift the cheeks, flash the brows, nod). "How fast we respond reflects how much we care." The Ben Franklin favor and the compliment that lets them praise themselves, from the interviewing book. "Is this annoying you?"
- The performer's application, which is where it stops being Joe's material: where to stand relative to a volunteer on stage and on which side, the walk-up to a table of strangers with open feet, approaching a woman so she sees you coming and with the extra foot, the head tilt while a volunteer answers, the brow flash on the reveal instead of the flat face after a miracle, and the handshake as the first baseline read of the night.

### Part Three: reading, Joe's way

This replaces the current Part Two. The Ten Commandments, comfort and discomfort, baseline through rapport, clusters, pacifiers, feet then hands then torso then face. Eye blocking, the lip purse as a trial balloon (float three options and watch the mouth, which is Chris's lip-compression steering cue made formal), the negative rule. The 54 percent ceiling and what you never claim. What this replaces: microexpressions as lie detection, NLP, DISC. Volunteer selection by comfort: shake and wait, the flash-frozen volunteer, the starter's position. The Five Cs and the three-signal rule survive as Chris's own.

**The neck (added 2026-10-27).** Joe's neck material from the cue index: the suprasternal notch cover (his high-value distress marker; the "Captured in the Neck of Time" case, where the fugitive's mother touched it three times when asked if her son was home), neck rubbing and massaging, the front-of-neck grasp, the neck touch timed to a specific question, collar ventilating, the pacifying pendulum between necklace and lap, lifting the hair off the nape, the neck flush on a personally loaded question (the "have you been sued" divorce story), the turtle effect, and the head tilt that exposes the neck as the cleanest comfort cue to give or receive. The sex difference: men grab or massage the neck, women touch the notch. In negotiation, any neck touching broadcasts weakness.

The platysmal bands are Chris's own. They appear nowhere in the Academy lessons, the extra readings, the Business Course or Mastering Connections. They are in his Elizabeth Holmes analysis (2026-10-22) and on the Decode Behavior demo, so the chapter presents them as his field observation, with the anatomy glossed in the sentence that names it (the platysma is the thin sheet of muscle under the skin of the front of the neck, and when it tightens the vertical cords stand out). The science anchor is Ekman's Facial Action Coding System, which lists it as Action Unit 21, the neck tightener. The honest framing is that it fires with fear, effort and vocal strain, which is why it showed on Holmes when she forced her voice down. For a performer the use is the volunteer who is bracing: the bands show before the face admits anything.
